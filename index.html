Why this site exists

Good care and correct billing come from the same place: fidelity.

The Collaborative Care Model works. Decades of research show that when a primary care clinician, a behavioral health care manager, and a consulting psychiatrist share a registry, measure symptoms over time, and adjust treatment for patients who aren’t improving, people get better. That is why BlueCross BlueShield of Tennessee covers CoCM, and why Tennessee now requires every third-party payer to reimburse the CoCM codes.

Coverage only helps patients if the model is delivered the way it was designed. This site, drawn from the ETSU–BCBST implementation guide and the ETSU pilot in East Tennessee, shows you how to do that: how to bill the codes so you get paid and follow the rules, how to build a registry that keeps every patient in view, and how to bring psychiatry into the team. The goal is to help this model spread across Tennessee with the quality that makes it worth paying for.

Billing for the CoCM codes

CoCM codes are billed once per calendar month, based on the total time the care team spends on a patient’s collaborative care. The time adds up across many short contacts, so your documentation has to capture every one.

Getting it right

  • Track cumulative CoCM time for each patient, per calendar month, as it happens.
  • Document the activities behind the time: outreach, follow-up visits, registry updates, and caseload review with the psychiatric consultant.
  • Confirm eligible billing practitioners, payer rules, and limits on billing other services in the same period.
  • Before you scale, send a small sample of claims (8–10) to BlueCross BlueShield of Tennessee for feedback, then refine your workflow.

Planning for sustainability

In 2025, Tennessee passed legislation requiring all third-party payers to reimburse the CoCM codes. Two formulas help you plan:

Expected revenue = Billable service volume × Expected payment per service
Break-even volume = Total program cost ÷ Average net revenue per billable episode

Adjust for payer mix, denials, and collection rates. Costs include BHCM and psychiatric consultant time, admin support, registry or technology, and training.

Section 2

Implementing a registry

↑ Back to top

The registry is what makes CoCM population-based. It lets your team see every enrolled patient at once and spot the ones who aren’t getting better. It doesn’t have to be fancy. It has to be used.

At minimum, your registry should

  • Identify every patient enrolled in CoCM.
  • Track clinical measures (such as the PHQ-9 or GAD-7) and follow-up over time.
  • Monitor engagement, so patients who drop off are noticed.
  • Flag patients who are not improving as expected.
  • Support systematic caseload review with the psychiatric consultant.

From the ETSU pilot

The ETSU team built its registry in REDCap while the organization was changing EHRs. It captured patient and behavioral health information, CoCM time, follow-up activity, psychiatric consultation, safety planning, and measures including the PHQ-A, GAD-7, CRAFFT+N, CORS, SRS, ACE, and PSC-17.

Tip: Capturing CoCM time in the registry lets you build billing triggers in, so you can see when a patient crosses a code threshold for the month.

Section 3

Engaging psychiatry

↑ Back to top

In CoCM, the psychiatric consultant supports the whole caseload instead of seeing every patient. Through regular review with the care manager, one psychiatrist’s expertise reaches many more people in primary care.

Plan these before you start

  1. Find the right consultantClinical expertise that fits your population, and protected time for caseload review.
  2. Set the rhythmDecide how often you’ll meet and in what format. Predictable is better than frequent.
  3. Prioritize the casesUse the registry to bring forward new patients and those not improving as expected.
  4. Close the loopDecide how recommendations are documented and passed to the PCP, who prescribes.
  5. Plan for coverageKnow who reviews cases when the consultant is unavailable.

How it worked in the ETSU pilot

The ETSU team partnered with a psychiatrist with extensive child and adolescent experience. Each week, the psychiatrist and the care manager met for a one-hour, HIPAA-compliant video conference. Physicians were invited to join and present their own patients when they could.

Build team buy-in. A formal kickoff, at a staff meeting or Grand Rounds, helps every clinician understand how and when to involve the care manager and the psychiatric consultant.

Each role, in brief: the PCP provides medical care and prescribes; the BHCM engages patients, tracks the registry, and coordinates; the psychiatric consultant reviews cases and recommends treatment changes.

Section 4

Frequently asked questions

↑ Back to top
What is the Collaborative Care Model?

CoCM is a team-based way to treat behavioral health conditions in primary care. The primary care clinician works with a behavioral health care manager and a psychiatric consultant to identify, monitor, and treat patients, using a registry and validated measures to track progress and catch patients who aren’t improving.

How is CoCM different from other integrated care models?

Primary Care Behavioral Health (PCBH) focuses on brief, same-day support for the whole clinic population. CoCM treats and tracks a defined group of patients with scheduled follow-up and structured psychiatric review. The two work well together, and many clinics use both.

Will BlueCross BlueShield of Tennessee pay for CoCM?

Yes. BCBST covers the CoCM codes, and in its 2025 session Tennessee passed legislation requiring all third-party payers to reimburse them. Before you scale, consider sending 8–10 sample encounters to BCBST to confirm your documentation and billing meet expectations.

Which conditions should we start with?

Start small. Many practices begin with depression because it is common in primary care, the PHQ-9 is a well-validated measure, and it fits the measurement-based approach CoCM billing requires. Expand once your workflows are solid.

Does the psychiatrist need to see every patient?

No. The consulting psychiatrist mainly reviews cases with the care manager and recommends treatment changes. The primary care clinician stays responsible for the patient’s care and prescribing.

Do we need an EHR-based registry?

No. A secure spreadsheet, REDCap, a purpose-built tool like the AIMS Caseload Tracker, or your EHR can all work, as long as the registry reliably tracks enrolled patients, measures, follow-up, and who isn’t improving. See Implementing a registry.

Can follow-up happen by phone or telehealth?

Yes, and it matters. Regular contact with the care manager is one of the strongest drivers of improvement. The ETSU pilot used a HIPAA-compliant telecommunication app for patient contact and video conferencing for psychiatric review. Confirm which channels your organization approves before building them into workflows.

What training does the care team need?

Everyone on the team should learn the principles and workflows of CoCM. Care managers typically complete the AIMS Center Behavioral Health Care Manager training. In the ETSU pilot, care managers also used the Meadows Mental Health Policy Institute Collaborative Care Training Hub and found the two complementary.

How will we know it’s working?

Track how patients move through the pathway: the share of eligible visits screened, the share of patients above threshold who are referred, and the share referred who enroll. Watch symptom scores over time for each patient, not just the average, so no one who is stuck gets missed.
